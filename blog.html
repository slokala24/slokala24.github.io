Why Electrical Engineers & Physicists Make the Best Systems Thinkers

There's a joke in software circles that firmware engineers are just software engineers who never learned to let go. But spend enough time in the stack — close enough to the silicon that you can feel the clock cycles — and you start to see the world differently.

The constraint is the point

Firmware engineering is, at its core, an exercise in constraint. You have limited memory. Limited compute. A real-time deadline that, if missed, doesn't just slow things down — it breaks something physical. A missed interrupt in a wireless chipset doesn't produce a slower UI. It drops a packet. It fails a handshake. The world responds.

This forces a discipline that higher-level development rarely demands: you must understand the full system. Not just your layer, but the hardware below you, the OS above you, the RF environment around you, and the human expectation at the end of it all.

Working close to the metal teaches you that everything is connected — and that abstraction, while powerful, always has a cost.

Why this transfers

When I moved into senior engineering roles — and later into an MBA program — I noticed something. The mental model I'd built from years of embedded systems work applied everywhere. Evaluating a startup's tech stack? I was naturally asking: where are the bottlenecks? What's the real-time constraint? What happens when this assumption breaks?

In business strategy, the same principle holds. Every system — organisational, financial, technological — has constraints. Most people reason from the top down. Firmware engineers reason from the bottom up. We ask: what does the hardware actually allow? What's the physical limit? What are we pretending doesn't matter?

The communication edge

There's a second advantage that's less obvious: firmware engineers tend to be excellent communicators — not despite the technical depth, but because of it. When you've had to explain an interrupt latency issue to a product manager, and a PCB layout constraint to a software team, and a power budget tradeoff to an executive, you develop range.

You learn that every audience needs a different frame. The fact stays the same. The entry point changes. That skill — meeting people where they are — is, I'd argue, the most underrated capability in any technical career.

What I'd tell a junior engineer

If you're early in a firmware or embedded career and wondering whether it's worth the depth — it is. Not because the job market rewards it (it does), but because the thinking it builds is transferable in ways you won't fully appreciate until you're in a boardroom explaining why the system can't do what the slide deck promised.

The metal teaches you truth. And truth, it turns out, is always useful.

What Evaluating 20+ Deep-Tech Startups Taught Me About Product-Market Fit

During my time as a Venture Analyst at the Creative Destruction Lab's Climate Stream at UBC, I had the opportunity to evaluate over twenty deep-tech and next-generation compute ventures. Each one had a compelling technology story. Far fewer had a compelling market story. Here's what I learned.

Technology readiness is necessary, not sufficient

The most common mistake I saw was founders conflating technical maturity with market readiness. A technology can be genuinely breakthrough — novel, defensible, peer-reviewed — and still have no clear path to adoption. The question isn't just "does it work?" but "who changes their behaviour to use it, and why now?"

Deep-tech founders, especially those coming from academia or large engineering organisations, are often excellent at answering the first question and surprisingly unprepared for the second.

The best founders I evaluated could explain their technology to a physicist and their business model to a CFO — in the same breath.

The PMF signal in deep-tech is different

In consumer software, product-market fit has a feel: retention, NPS, the "pull" of organic growth. In deep-tech, it looks different. It looks like a design win with a lighthouse customer. It looks like a procurement officer who's already argued internally for your budget line. It looks like a regulatory pathway that's been mapped, not assumed.

When I saw these signals — even early, even imperfectly — the ventures tended to have legs. When founders were still in "we need to educate the market" mode at the seed stage, that was a yellow flag.

What separates the survivors

Across all the ventures I reviewed, the ones that progressed shared a few traits. First, they had a crisp answer to "why us, why now." Not just a technology advantage — a timing argument. Why is this the moment this technology becomes viable or necessary?

Second, they understood their customer's decision-making process. Not just the end user, but the procurement chain, the budget holder, the technical gatekeeper. Deep-tech sales cycles are long and involve many nodes. Founders who'd already mapped that chain — even theoretically — were miles ahead.

Third, they were honest about what they didn't know. Paradoxically, the founders who acknowledged gaps in their knowledge of regulation, manufacturing scale, or market structure inspired more confidence, not less. It signalled self-awareness and a capacity to learn.

What I carry forward

This experience shaped how I think about my own venture, Entoq, and how I advise others. Technology is the entry fee. The game is market understanding, timing, and execution. The engineers who learn to think like operators — without losing their technical rigour — are the ones who build something that lasts.

The Art of Explaining Hard Things Simply

I've taught engineering concepts to a fourteen-year-old who thought electricity was magic, and to a senior engineer who'd been in the field for two decades but never quite understood why certain RF interference patterns emerge. Both conversations required the same fundamental skill: knowing where someone actually is, not where you think they should be.

The curse of knowledge

The biggest obstacle to clear explanation isn't intelligence — it's familiarity. When you've understood something for long enough, it stops feeling like something you learned. It just feels true. And when you try to explain it, you skip the steps that once felt hard, because they no longer feel hard to you.

This is what cognitive scientists call the "curse of knowledge." The antidote isn't simplifying your content — it's rebuilding the journey. What did you not know before you understood this? Start there.

Good teaching isn't about what you know. It's about remembering what it felt like not to know it.

Analogies are load-bearing

The right analogy does more work than a perfect technical definition. When I explain interrupt-driven programming to a non-technical stakeholder, I don't talk about vector tables or latency. I say: imagine a chef who only checks the oven when a timer goes off, instead of opening it every thirty seconds. That's interrupt-driven. That's efficient. That's why your phone battery lasts a day.

Analogies aren't dumbing things down. They're building a bridge from what someone already understands to what they're trying to understand. The trick is choosing an analogy that doesn't break down the moment you push on it.

Layers, not levels

One pattern I've found consistently useful: explain in layers, not levels. Don't give a beginner explanation and an expert explanation as if they're different things. Give the same explanation at increasing resolution. Start with the shape of the idea. Then add the texture. Then the edge cases.

This way, a listener can exit the explanation at whatever depth serves them — and come back for more when they're ready. It respects both their time and their intelligence.

The payoff

The ability to explain clearly isn't just a teaching skill. It's a leadership skill, a sales skill, a strategy skill. In every senior role I've held, the moments that moved projects forward weren't the moments I demonstrated the most technical depth. They were the moments I made someone in the room feel like they finally understood something that had been fuzzy.

Clarity creates momentum. And momentum, in any organisation, is worth more than almost anything else.

From Engineer to MBA: What Business School Actually Teaches You

I came into the UBC Sauder MBA with twelve years of engineering experience, a strong opinion about what I didn't need to learn, and a vague sense that business school was mostly about networking and frameworks with impressive names. I was wrong about most of that.

What I expected vs. what I found

I expected the MBA to be a credential play — something that would open doors that a technical resume couldn't. That turned out to be partially true, but it was the least interesting thing I got from it.

What I didn't expect was how much the structured exposure to finance, strategy, and organisational behaviour would change my internal model of how value gets created and captured. Engineers are trained to optimise within a defined system. The MBA taught me to question whether you're optimising the right system at all.

The most valuable thing business school taught me was to ask "what problem are we actually solving?" — before touching any solution.

What's genuinely useful for engineers

Corporate finance, done seriously, changes how you think about investment and risk. Not in an abstract way — in a concrete, "this is how a board evaluates a technical bet" way. For engineers who want to lead, or who want to found something, this is invaluable.

Entrepreneurial finance was where theory met reality for me. Valuation, term sheets, cap table mechanics — none of it is magic, but it's opaque if you've never been taught it. Understanding the financial language of startups made me a better participant in CDL and a better co-founder at Entoq.

Marketing — which I'll admit I approached with the most skepticism — turned out to be the most consistently useful course. Not the brand-building and advertising version, but the positioning and segmentation version. Who exactly is the customer? What do they believe before they encounter you? What needs to shift? These questions matter in engineering, too — we just don't usually ask them explicitly.

What I'd skip

Some of the softer leadership and communication modules felt designed for people who had never managed anyone or run a meeting. For engineers with a decade of cross-functional experience, this content was often redundant. That's not a criticism of the program — it's a calibration note for experienced professionals considering an MBA.

The honest bottom line

The MBA is worth it if you use it actively — not as a passive credential, but as a lens. Every framework, every case study, every finance model is an invitation to look at your existing experience differently. The engineers who get the most from business school are the ones who show up willing to have their assumptions challenged. I'd like to think I was one of them.
